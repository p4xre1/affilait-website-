Keyword research is a way to learn how people describe a problem. It is not a publishing quota. Semrush can help you collect possible topics and organize them; your job is to decide which questions deserve a useful answer from your site.

This guide is a research-led walkthrough, not a hands-on product test. Tool names, limits, and available features can change, so check the current Semrush interface and account plan as you follow along.

## 1. Start with a reader and a task

Write down who the page is for and what they are trying to do. “People interested in SEO” is too broad. “A university editor updating a program page” or “a new publisher planning a beginner keyword lesson” gives your research a useful boundary.

Choose a seed phrase that describes the task in ordinary language. Keep a short note about why the topic belongs on your site before opening a tool.

## 2. Explore ideas in Keyword Magic Tool

Enter the seed phrase in Semrush's Keyword Magic Tool and select the database that matches the audience you intend to serve. Explore related phrases and questions. Treat search volume, keyword difficulty, and intent labels as directional signals, not exact forecasts.

Save only ideas that connect to the reader's task. A long list of loosely related terms is not a content plan.

## 3. Review your own search visibility

If the site already has search history, Semrush's Organic Rankings report can help identify queries where your pages are already visible. Those may reveal an existing page to improve or an audience question you have not answered clearly.

For first-party performance on your own verified site, compare the report with Google Search Console. Different tools use different data sources and estimates, so the numbers will not always match.

## 4. Read the search results before choosing a page

Search the most promising phrases yourself. Look at the kinds of results that appear, what they answer, and whether they match the page you planned to write. Notice whether searchers seem to want a definition, a step-by-step lesson, a comparison, or an official program page.

If the results point to different needs, do not force every phrase onto one page. If several phrases share the same intent, one thorough page may serve them better than several near-duplicates.

## 5. Cluster ideas, then write an editorial brief

Use a keyword list or cluster as a planning aid. For each proposed page, record:

- The intended reader and the question they need answered
- The page's primary purpose and the related phrases it will naturally address
- The evidence, expert review, or official sources required
- What the page must not claim or imply
- Who will review accuracy and when key facts should be checked again

This brief should guide a writer, not dictate exact wording or a target number of keyword mentions.

## 6. Measure the result after publishing

After the page has been live long enough to gather data, review impressions, clicks, engagement, and the actions that matter to the site. Use the results to find questions to clarify or pages to improve. A tool estimate alone cannot tell you whether a page helped a reader.

## Common questions

### Should I choose the highest-volume keyword?

Not automatically. Relevance, intent, competition, and your ability to provide a better answer matter too. A smaller, highly relevant question can be more useful than a broad phrase that attracts the wrong audience.

### Should I create one page for every keyword?

No. Group related phrases only when the same page can answer the underlying need well. Creating thin pages just to target small wording variations can make a site less helpful.

## The Fatorati takeaway

Semrush is useful for expanding and organizing the research process. The strongest content plan still starts with a real audience, checks the live search results, and gives an editor the final say.
