Education websites serve people at different stages: prospective students, current learners, families, faculty, and researchers. Their search questions are not interchangeable. A useful SEO plan starts by deciding whose question a page is meant to answer.

This is an editorial planning guide, not admissions advice or a guarantee of search performance. Each institution should have its academic and admissions teams verify important details before publication.

## 1. Separate audiences before doing keyword research

Choose one audience and one task for each research pass. Someone comparing programs may need curriculum and entry requirements. A current student may need a course resource or an explanation of a subject. A family member may be trying to understand application dates or support services.

Use Semrush to explore phrases related to that task and the country or market the page serves. Save ideas only when they match a real program, service, or learning resource the institution offers.

## 2. Build useful pages around real offerings

A program page should describe the actual program, not a generic template filled with keyword variations. Depending on the institution, useful details may include the curriculum, delivery format, location, entry requirements, dates, costs, and a clear route to official admissions information.

A student guidance article can answer a question that appears across multiple programs, but it should link readers to the official page for the decision-specific details. Do not invent course names, locations, outcomes, or application steps to match a search term.

## 3. Use search data as a research prompt

Keyword metrics can help an editor compare possible topics. They cannot tell you whether a course is available, whether an application date is current, or whether a claim meets the institution's standards. Check those details with the staff responsible for them and cite primary sources where appropriate.

Review the current search results too. They can show whether people are looking for an official program page, an explainer, a comparison, or another kind of resource. That observation helps choose the page format; it does not replace interviews with students or academic expertise.

## 4. Add an accuracy and accessibility review

Before publication, name a subject-matter reviewer and an owner for time-sensitive details. Check that the page is readable on mobile, uses clear headings, explains unfamiliar terms, and offers an accessible route to the next step.

For admissions, tuition, accreditation, and other high-impact information, add a visible review date and a process for keeping the page current. Search performance should never be a reason to obscure important caveats.

## 5. Measure what helps learners

After publication, use first-party site data and feedback to see whether visitors find the right information. Look beyond rankings: are people reaching the official program details, completing an intended task, or asking fewer avoidable questions? Do not treat visits or applications as proof that the content itself is accurate or useful.

## The Fatorati takeaway

Semrush can help education teams discover search language and organize research. The institution remains responsible for the facts, the academic standard, and the learner's experience.
